Can a community in a link network also be recognised in the words used to describe its members?

The Week 8 takeaway

Find data you can inspect. Then sharpen the question.

The official guidance, sections 5, 6 and 8, asks us to find accessible data with both links and text, inspect a sample, and choose a question that the two can answer together. A few hundred to a few thousand nodes is a manageable starting point. Methods need checks, and each figure needs a purpose.

The preparation ends with one slide: question; dataset and size; network and text measurements; a check and a reason to abandon the idea; and a figure sketch. The group still needs to choose the idea together and confirm its registration on DTU Learn. This recap does not claim either has happened.

Proposed · One question

Do link communities share biographical vocabulary beyond what their era explains?

Specifically: are philosophers in the same Wikipedia link community described with more similar words than expected when descriptions are shuffled among philosophers from the same era?

Candidate dataset and representation
SourceOfficial frozen philosophers dataset, 15 September 2026: English Wikipedia list members born before 1900. Existing node TSV and edge TSV.
Nodes1,444 philosopher pages, joined by exact node_id. Retain the full roster, including isolates.
EdgesA → B means A’s article links to B’s. For this candidate, combine both directions into one unweighted edge and remove self-links. Repeated links will not add weight.
TextThe node TSV’s description field: a short biographical blurb per philosopher, not a full article. No new crawl or cross-date join is needed. The era field identifies a century-list category, not an exact birth year.

Observed · Dataset check

Both halves are present. The text is thin.

A standard-library Python check run on 24 November 2026 found 1,444 unique node IDs, 11,135 directed edge rows, and 1,444 nonempty descriptions and era labels. All edge endpoints exist in the roster; there are no duplicate directed pairs or self-links, and all weights are positive integers. 68 nodes have no incident edge.

Description lengths range from 6 to 56 words, with a median of 17, counting Unicode letter runs and splitting hyphens. For example, Abhinavagupta’s stored blurb mentions a philosopher, mystic and aesthetician from Kashmir; Abraham Tucker’s describes an English country gentleman studying philosophy. These illustrate biographical content, not comprehensive accounts of their ideas.

Consequence: the data support a small vocabulary pilot. They do not yet justify a claim about shared philosophical beliefs. Full prose would be a later extension requiring a documented collection date and a checked join.

Proposed · Two methods, one answer

Use links to form groups, then text to test them.

Network method: run unweighted Louvain at resolution 1, with seeds 0–19, on the same full graph. This finds groups with relatively dense internal links; it does not identify certified schools of thought. Isolates remain in the graph, but singleton communities have no within-group pairs.

Text method: lowercase the descriptions, remove each node’s own name tokens and English stopwords, and fit TF–IDF with a minimum document frequency of 2. Measure cosine similarity for every unordered pair within a non-singleton community. Average over these pairs to get one score per partition; report that large communities contribute more pairs. Record and exclude zero-vector descriptions from both the observed and shuffled scores.

TF–IDF highlights words that distinguish documents within this corpus; cosine measures weighted vocabulary overlap, not agreement or influence. The combined measurement asks whether groups formed from links also share descriptive vocabulary.

Validation and a stopping rule

Not yet run: keep each partition fixed and shuffle eligible description vectors among nodes within each era category, 1,000 times with random seed 42. Compare the actual score with this null distribution; it preserves era-specific vocabulary while breaking its assignment to individuals. Repeat for all 20 Louvain seeds.

If the actual score stays inside the central 95% of its null distribution across seeds, we would drop the claim that these communities share vocabulary beyond era and revise the question. Also inspect 20 seeded sample pairs against their source blurbs, including high- and low-similarity cases. Nationality, occupation and short-text boilerplate can still explain similarity; an era shuffle does not control those factors.

Proposed · Figure and next decision

Put the check beside the claim.

Figure idea, not a result: a horizontal dot-and-interval plot with one row per Louvain seed. The x-axis is mean within-community cosine similarity; a coloured dot shows the actual score and a grey interval shows the central 95% of the era-shuffled scores. Readers could immediately see whether any apparent effect survives the comparison and changes of seed. No simulated result is displayed here.

Before adopting this candidate, the group should decide whether short biographical descriptions answer a question it cares about. Coverage is restricted to Wikipedia’s pre-1900 lists, links reflect editorial choices, and era labels are coarse. The next step is the proposed pilot and pair inspection—not a claim that language and communities already agree.
